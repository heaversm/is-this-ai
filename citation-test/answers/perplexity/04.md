Probably not. Blue-light-filtering glasses haven’t consistently reduced screen-related eye strain compared with regular lenses. Some people find them more comfortable, but the evidence doesn’t support paying extra for a blue-light filter specifically to treat eye strain.

Why screens make your eyes tired

Screen discomfort is more often related to reduced blinking, dry eyes, and prolonged close-up focusing—not the blue light itself. An outdated glasses prescription can also contribute. Blue-light filters don’t address those underlying problems. [clevelandclinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/do-blue-light-blocking-glasses-actually-work)

If a particular pair feels helpful, its tint or anti-reflective coating may be improving comfort rather than the blue-light filtering. That distinction matters when choosing glasses. [clevelandclinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/do-blue-light-blocking-glasses-actually-work)

What to try instead
- Take regular breaks. Use the 20-20-20 rule as a reminder: every 20 minutes, look about 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
- Blink deliberately. If your eyes feel dry or gritty, artificial tears may help; choose preservative-free drops if you need them more than three or four times daily.
- Adjust your setup. Keep your monitor about an arm’s length away and enlarge text so you aren’t squinting or leaning forward.
- Get an eye exam if symptoms persist. You may need an updated prescription or treatment for dry eyes rather than a special lens filter. [clevelandclinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/do-blue-light-blocking-glasses-actually-work)

Sleep is a separate question: evening blue-light exposure can affect your body clock, but evidence that blue-light glasses improve sleep remains mixed. That possible use shouldn’t be confused with relief from eye strain. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12833160/)
