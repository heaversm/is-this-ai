The sky is blue because air molecules scatter blue sunlight more strongly than red sunlight, sending blue light toward your eyes from all across the sky. This is called Rayleigh scattering. [weather.metoffice.gov](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/optical-effects/why-is-the-sky-blue)

How it works

Sunlight looks white, but it contains all the colors of the rainbow. When it enters Earth’s atmosphere, nitrogen and oxygen molecules scatter some of that light—changing its direction. Shorter wavelengths, such as blue, scatter much more strongly than longer wavelengths, such as red. So when you look away from the Sun, the light reaching you from the sky is especially rich in blue.

Why not violet?

Violet has an even shorter wavelength and scatters more strongly than blue. But sunlight contains less violet, and our eyes are less sensitive to it, so we perceive the sky as blue rather than violet. [weather.metoffice.gov](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/optical-effects/why-is-the-sky-blue)

Why sunsets turn red

At sunset, sunlight travels through a longer stretch of atmosphere before reaching you. Much of the blue light gets scattered out of that direct path, leaving a greater proportion of red and orange light. It’s the same scattering process, seen through more air.
