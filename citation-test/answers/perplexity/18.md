The latest UN climate summit is COP31, underway in Antalya, Türkiye. As of November 12, 2026, it has not finished: it opened November 9 and is scheduled to close November 20. So far, it has produced new funding announcements and urgent appeals from vulnerable countries—but no final negotiated agreement yet.

What has happened so far?
- Leaders pressed for faster action, but major emitters’ leaders stayed away. More than 70 world leaders and senior officials participated in the leaders’ summit. The leaders of China, the United States and India—countries that together account for more than half of global carbon dioxide emissions—did not attend. UN Secretary-General António Guterres urged countries to reduce fossil-fuel dependence, strengthen adaptation and increase financial support for poorer countries.
- Vulnerable countries demanded money and protection. Small-island leaders described climate change as a life-or-death issue and called for stronger funding to address climate damage. Negotiations remain divided over adaptation finance—money to help communities cope with warming—and the transition away from fossil fuels.
- New funding was announced, though that is different from a summit-wide deal. Philanthropic organizations announced more than $480 million to tackle methane and other powerful greenhouse gases, plus another $25 million to support emissions reductions in seven countries. A new UN disaster-risk financing platform aims to unlock more than $3 billion to protect 75 million people by 2030; that is a target, not money already delivered. - Cities and resilience received greater attention. For the first time at a UN climate conference, the leaders’ summit devoted a high-level dialogue to housing, urban resilience and climate action—bringing protection of homes and communities into the spotlight alongside emissions cuts. What is the main unresolved issue?

The central question is whether governments will turn earlier commitments into concrete, financed action. Türkiye is promoting a goal of raising electricity’s share of global energy use to 35% by 2035. But critics point out that electrification alone does not guarantee emissions reductions: the proposal does not require the additional electricity to come from clean sources. A broader agreement on moving away from coal, oil and gas remains contested. The story so far is therefore one of practical funding announcements and stronger demands for action, while the biggest collective decisions—on finance, emissions and fossil fuels—are still being negotiated. It is too early to describe COP31 as a completed breakthrough or failure.
