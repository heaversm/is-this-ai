No—not on average. Intermittent fasting generally produces about the same weight loss as conventional dieting that reduces calories every day. It can be a useful approach, but it has not proved consistently better. A 2026 Cochrane review of 22 randomized trials involving nearly 2,000 adults found little to no difference in weight loss compared with traditional dietary advice.

Why it can work

Intermittent fasting changes when you eat—for example, limiting meals to an eight-hour window or sharply reducing intake on two days each week. Conventional calorie restriction focuses on eating less each day. Both can help you lose weight by reducing total calorie intake; a fasting window does not guarantee weight loss if you compensate by eating more during it.

There is one nuance: an analysis of 99 trials found that alternate-day fasting produced about 1.3 kg—roughly 3 pounds—more weight loss than daily calorie restriction. That was a small difference, below the study’s threshold for a clinically meaningful advantage, and longer trials are needed. It does not establish that a typical daily eating window, such as 16:8, is superior. [sciencedaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/06/250619090901.htm)

Which approach makes more sense?

The practical choice is the approach you can sustain while eating adequately—not the one with the longest fasting period. Research has not established that fasting is consistently easier to follow or better at controlling appetite. [nutritionsource.hsph.harvard](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-weight/diet-reviews/intermittent-fasting/)
- Fasting may suit you if having a clear eating schedule feels simpler than monitoring portions throughout the day.
- Daily calorie reduction may suit you better if skipping meals leaves you hungry or makes you overeat later.

Those are reasons to choose based on your own response, rather than expecting a special weight-loss advantage from fasting. Long-term comparisons remain limited, particularly beyond a year. [nutritionsource.hsph.harvard](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-weight/diet-reviews/intermittent-fasting/)

A safety caveat

Check with a clinician before trying fasting if you have diabetes—especially if you use insulin—are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of an eating disorder. Extended fasts are not necessarily more effective and can be dangerous. [hopkinsmedicine](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/expert-qa/intermittent-fasting-what-is-it-and-how-does-it-work)
