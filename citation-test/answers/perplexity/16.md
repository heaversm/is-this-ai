Global sea level is rising by roughly 4–5 millimeters per year—about 0.16–0.20 inches annually, or roughly 1.6–2 inches per decade at that pace. The rate is accelerating: satellite-based estimates put the average at about 4.5 mm per year during 2011–2020, more than twice the typical rate during the twentieth century.

What’s driving the rise?

Two main processes are raising the ocean’s average height:
- Warming seawater expands, taking up more space.
- Melting glaciers and ice sheets add water to the ocean.

Human-caused warming is the dominant driver of global sea-level rise since 1970.

The cumulative change matters as much as the annual rate: global average sea level has risen about 8–9 inches since 1880. A higher baseline lets high tides and storm surges reach farther inland, increasing coastal flooding.

Why the rate differs by location

The global average isn’t what every coastline experiences. Ocean currents and winds redistribute water, while the land itself can sink or rise. A sinking coastline can experience faster relative sea-level rise; rising land can partly offset it. Some parts of the Pacific Northwest have even experienced falling local sea levels despite the global increase. And “2 inches per decade” is a description of today’s approximate pace—not a fixed forecast. Because the rate is accelerating, simply multiplying it by the number of decades remaining would underestimate future rise if that acceleration continues.
