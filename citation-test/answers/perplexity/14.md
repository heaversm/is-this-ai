Yes—battery-electric cars generally produce fewer lifetime greenhouse-gas emissions than comparable gasoline cars, even after accounting for battery manufacturing and the electricity used to charge them. They are not emissions-free, but their lower emissions during driving usually more than offset their higher manufacturing footprint.

What a lifetime comparison includes

A fair comparison counts the entire vehicle life cycle—not just what comes out of the tailpipe: raw-material extraction, vehicle and battery manufacturing, fuel or electricity production, driving, and eventual recycling or disposal. Gasoline’s footprint includes oil extraction, refining, and transportation, not just burning it in the engine. [afdc.energy](https://afdc.energy.gov/vehicles/electric-emissions)

The balance works like this:
- Manufacturing: Making an EV usually produces more emissions than making a comparable gas car, largely because of battery production. [factcheck](https://www.factcheck.org/2024/02/electric-vehicles-contribute-fewer-emissions-than-gasoline-powered-cars-over-their-lifetimes/)
- Driving: An EV has no tailpipe emissions, but charging can cause emissions at power plants. Those operating emissions are generally much lower than the emissions associated with running a gasoline car.
- Over time: The savings during driving outweigh the extra manufacturing emissions. One analysis of an average EV produced in the U.S. in 2023 estimated that crossover at about 25,000 miles, or 2.2 years of driving. That is an illustrative estimate, not a universal cutoff. [scientificamerican](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/electric-vehicles-beat-gas-cars-on-climate-emissions-over-time/)

How much lower are emissions?

A 2021 International Council on Clean Transportation analysis estimated that average medium-size battery-electric cars had lifetime emissions 60–68% lower than gasoline counterparts in the United States. The estimated advantage varied by region: 66–69% in Europe, 37–45% in China, and 19–34% in India, reflecting differences in electricity supplies and other assumptions. These are study-specific estimates, not guarantees for every vehicle. [factcheck](https://www.factcheck.org/2024/02/electric-vehicles-contribute-fewer-emissions-than-gasoline-powered-cars-over-their-lifetimes/)

The biggest factors affecting an individual comparison are:
- Electricity source: Cleaner electricity increases the EV’s advantage; coal-heavy electricity reduces it. [afdc.energy](https://afdc.energy.gov/vehicles/electric-emissions)
- Vehicle and battery size: Size and model matter, so comparing similarly sized vehicles is more informative than comparing a large electric SUV with a small gasoline car. [factcheck](https://www.factcheck.org/2024/02/electric-vehicles-contribute-fewer-emissions-than-gasoline-powered-cars-over-their-lifetimes/)
- Lifetime mileage: Driving more miles gives the EV more opportunity to offset its higher manufacturing emissions.

Does “lower emissions” mean no environmental impact?

No. The conclusion is strongest for climate-changing greenhouse gases. EVs still have manufacturing impacts, and electricity generation can produce air pollution; “zero tailpipe emissions” does not mean “zero life-cycle emissions.”

For the question of a comparable new electric car versus a new gasoline car, though, the evidence consistently favors the electric car on lifetime climate emissions—not merely on tailpipe emissions.
