Some studies have linked artificial sweeteners to cancer, but the overall evidence does not establish that they cause cancer in humans. Most human studies have found no increase in cancer risk; a 2025 review combining previous meta-analyses likewise found no significant association with cancer overall. That does not prove zero risk, but it is more reassuring than many headlines suggest.

Why aspartame made headlines

In 2023, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified aspartame as “possibly carcinogenic to humans,” based on limited evidence, particularly concerning liver cancer. “Possibly” means the evidence raises a question—not that researchers have demonstrated that ordinary consumption causes cancer. The human studies were inconsistent. [cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/diet/artificial-sweeteners-fact-sheet)

A separate WHO/FAO expert committee reviewed the risk from consuming aspartame and kept its existing acceptable daily intake unchanged. The FDA also disagreed that the evidence justified the “possibly carcinogenic” classification. These assessments address different questions: whether a substance might cause cancer under some circumstances versus the risk posed by the amounts people consume. [cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/diet/artificial-sweeteners-fact-sheet)

Why a “link” isn’t proof

Much of this research is observational: researchers track what people consume and which illnesses they develop. People who use sweeteners may also differ in weight, diabetes status, diet, and other factors that affect cancer risk. Researchers can adjust for these differences, but cannot necessarily eliminate them. A small association in one study therefore needs confirmation across other studies—not just a plausible headline. [cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/diet/artificial-sweeteners-fact-sheet)

For everyday decisions, current evidence does not provide a strong cancer-related reason to avoid an occasional diet drink or sweetener. Water and unsweetened drinks remain straightforward options if you want to reduce sweetener consumption, but replacing diet drinks with sugary ones is not a demonstrated cancer-prevention strategy.
