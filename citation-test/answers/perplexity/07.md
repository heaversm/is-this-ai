For most healthy adults, a useful daily guide is 9–13 cups of fluids—roughly 2–3 liters—from water and other drinks. The general estimates are about 9 cups for women and 13 cups for men, but these are guides, not amounts everyone must force themselves to drink. [nutritionsource.hsph.harvard](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/water/)

What counts toward that amount?

Water, tea, coffee, and milk all count. Food also supplies water—typically about 20% of your total intake. Including both food and beverages, commonly cited daily estimates are: [mayoclinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/water/art-20044256)AdultsTotal water from food and drinksWomenAbout 2.7 liters, or 11.5 cupsMenAbout 3.7 liters, or 15.5 cups

These totals do not mean you need to drink that much plain water in addition to everything else you consume. [mdanderson](https://www.mdanderson.org/cancerwise/how-much-water-should-you-drink-a-day.h00-159778812.html)

How to adjust for your needs
- Use thirst and pale-yellow urine as practical guides rather than chasing an exact number.
- Drink more when you sweat through exercise or hot weather, and during pregnancy or breastfeeding. Illness can also increase fluid needs. [www](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/food-guidelines-and-food-labels/water-drinks-nutrition/)
- If you have a prescribed fluid restriction, or a heart, kidney, or liver condition, follow your clinician’s guidance rather than a general target. [harvardhealth](https://www.harvardhealth.org/staying-healthy/how-much-water-should-you-drink)

The familiar “eight glasses a day” rule is a reasonable starting point for some people, but it isn’t a universal requirement. [mayoclinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/water/art-20044256)
