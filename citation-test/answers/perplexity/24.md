The January 2025 Los Angeles wildfires had separate ignition sources, but the same conditions turned them into a disaster: extremely dry vegetation, powerful Santa Ana winds, and neighborhoods bordering fire-prone terrain. For the two largest fires, federal authorities have linked the Palisades Fire to an allegedly deliberately set earlier fire, and the Eaton Fire to Southern California Edison’s electrical infrastructure. Those allegations should be distinguished from court-proven responsibility.

What started the major fires?
- Palisades Fire: Federal investigators determined that it was a “holdover” from the smaller Lachman Fire, which began early on January 1. Although firefighters suppressed that blaze, it continued smoldering underground in vegetation’s roots. Strong winds on January 7 brought it back above ground, where it spread. Prosecutors charged Jonathan Rinderknecht with deliberately setting the original fire; the charges are allegations, not themselves proof of guilt. This later finding superseded early speculation about fireworks.
- Eaton Fire: The U.S. government’s September 2025 lawsuit alleged that faulty Southern California Edison power infrastructure ignited the fire on January 7. The complaint cited a transmission-line fault around the time of ignition and Edison’s statement that it was “not aware of evidence pointing to another possible source of ignition.” These are allegations in a civil case, rather than a final judicial determination of liability. Why did they spread so fiercely?

The key was a sequence of conditions—not simply a single spark:
- Wet winters grew the fuel. Back-to-back wet winters encouraged abundant grass and brush growth around Los Angeles. [climate](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles)
- Heat and a record-dry fall dried it out. That vegetation became highly flammable, leaving the landscape primed for explosive fire. [climate](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles)
- Extreme winds drove the flames. An unusually strong Santa Ana wind event then overlapped with those dry fuels, enabling rapid fire spread. [climate](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles)
- Homes stood directly beside flammable landscapes. Development along the boundary between neighborhoods and grass-and-shrub-covered hills put many buildings directly in the fires’ path, magnifying the destruction. [climate](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles)

What role did climate change play?

Climate change was a contributing risk factor, not the ignition source. NOAA’s assessment explains that warming helped create the hot, dry conditions that made vegetation more combustible. A preliminary attribution analysis found that human-caused warming and the developing La Niña both increased the likelihood and severity of the extreme fire-weather conditions. That does not mean climate change started either fire—or that it alone explains the disaster. [climate](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles)

The useful distinction is between what lit the fires and what made them catastrophic: investigators identified specific human-related ignition mechanisms, while weather, dried vegetation, and the location of homes determined how devastating those ignitions became.
